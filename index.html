    Appendix

    Methodology & limitations

    This is a proof of concept built entirely from public data, not a client deliverable. It costs nothing to run and is designed to be refreshed monthly. Recommendations are illustrative: they show how the evidence would be turned into advice.

    1 · CollectGDELT news records via Google BigQuery; Google Trends search interest.
    2 · CleanMatch brands, remove lookalikes, collapse syndicated copies, normalise for news volume.
    3 · AnalyseSpikes, tone, issues, outlets and voices; a blind headline review checks the automation.
    4 · ScoreA weekly Reputation Risk Index and an issue priority score.
    5 · RecommendPrioritised actions with an owner, timing and the evidence behind them.

    Data sources

    • News: the GDELT Global Knowledge Graph, queried on Google BigQuery. One record per online news article: date, outlet, URL, headline, tone, and the organisations and people named. English-language originals only.
    • Search: Google Trends, US, weekly, using Google's disambiguated topics (e.g. "Cava Group — restaurant chain") rather than raw words.
    • Headline review: a stratified random sample of 500 headlines (100 per brand), reviewed blind by Claude for relevance, sentiment toward the brand (−2 to +2) and issues.

    Definitions

    • About the brand: the brand is in the headline, or named 3+ times in the article. This removes roundups and passing mentions; only 22–46% of airline mentions qualify.
    • Syndication: the same headline on the same outlet on the same day counts once. Collapsing iHeartMedia's copies across local station sites removed 23% of records.
    • Per 100k: brand articles per 100,000 English articles GDELT processed in the period, so busy and quiet news weeks are comparable.
    • Tone: GDELT's dictionary-based score of positive vs negative words. "Negative" means a tone below −2.
    • Issues: 14 categories assigned by transparent keyword rules on headlines (an article can have several). Outlet types come from domain lists and broadcast call signs.
    • Search interest: relative (0–100 within one request). Brands are compared only when they were pulled together.

    The scoring layer

    • Reputation Risk Index (0–100, weekly): 50% attention (coverage vs the brand's previous 8 weeks, log scale, 16× normal = maximum), 25% negativity (share of articles with tone below −2), 25% severity (the most serious issue in each headline, averaged). It uses only information available at the time, so it can be backtested.

      Limitations

      • Headlines, not full text. Issue tags and sentiment are based on headlines and named entities, not article bodies.
      • Coverage is broad but not complete. GDELT leans towards online news and local TV and radio sites; paywalled national outlets are under-represented. Read volumes as relative (brand vs brand, month vs month), not as counts of all coverage.
      • Tone understates bad news. Tone lines show direction of travel, not the size of the reputational damage.
      • The risk index is a triage tool. It flags where to look, not how bad it is: a big positive launch in an incident week can reach Watch. Quiet weeks with fewer than 5 articles score as normal, even before a crisis.
      • Restaurant news is mostly financial press, so for Cava and Dutch Bros, news share of voice largely reflects investor attention; search is the better consumer signal.
      • Small samples where noted. The headline review is one model's second opinion on 500 headlines, not human-coded ground truth. Some sub-results rest on 20–30 headlines and are labelled directional.
      • No social media. Reddit and other social platforms were out of scope for this proof of concept.
      • Roles of named people are shown only where verified; other names are as GDELT extracted them.
      • November 2026 is a partial month.
