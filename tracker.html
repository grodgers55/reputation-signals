One bad day defines Frontier. Southwest writes its own headlines.

Twelve months of news and search data on Frontier, Spirit and Southwest: where each airline's reputation was exposed, how early the trouble showed, and what each should do next.

What we found

58% of Frontier's coverage was about safety incidents, and every one of its five Alert weeks was an incident.

Southwest's coverage is driven by its own changes (seating, bags, policies, loyalty). Spirit's collapse showed in the data before it happened: the risk index put Spirit on Alert from the week ending 19 April, 13 days before it stopped flying.

What it means

Frontier's next incident will land on a brand with almost no positive news to balance it. Southwest's risk is self-inflicted, which also makes it controllable.

Automated tone scores understate all of this: a headline-by-headline review rated 85% of Spirit headlines negative, against 49% by GDELT tone.

What we recommend

Frontier: keep an incident-response kit ready, and use the first-class launch to build a steady product story.

Southwest: launch every policy change with a "what passengers gain" story.

All: monitor weekly with the risk index below, and check headlines, not just tone.

Analyst note · reading the index

Attention (50%): this week's coverage vs the brand's previous 8 weeks, on a log scale. Negativity (25%): share of articles with tone below −2. Severity (25%): how serious the issues in the headlines are (safety and bankruptcy count most).

The index tells you where to look, not how bad it is. Frontier's latest week sits just under Alert because its first-class launch made big news in the same week as a cabin-flood diversion. An analyst reads the headlines before anyone acts.
